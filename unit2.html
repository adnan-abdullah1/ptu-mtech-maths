    Unit 2

    Continuous Random Variables
    & Probability Density Functions

    When outcomes can't be counted, probability works differently.

    The shift

    From countable to uncountable

    In Unit 1, every random variable had values you could list out: 0, 1, 2, 3. Now consider measuring the height of a randomly selected student.

    Possible values could be:

    165 cm
    165.5 cm
    165.52 cm
    165.523 cm
    170.25 cm

    Between 165 cm and 166 cm alone, there are infinitely many possible values. You can't list them. This is a continuous random variable.

    ExperimentRandom variableType
    Measuring heightHeight in cmContinuous
    Measuring weightWeight in kgContinuous
    Measuring temperatureTemperatureContinuous
    Measuring waiting timeTime in secondsContinuous
    Rolling a diceNumber appearingDiscrete
    Counting customersNumber of customersDiscrete

    This sounds wrong. Does nobody have a height of exactly 170 cm?

    People do. But among infinitely many possible values, the probability of landing on one exact value is zero. Instead, we ask questions like:

    "What's the probability that height falls between 169 and 171 cm?" This can be greater than zero.

    The fundamental difference Discrete: probabilities are assigned to individual values.
    Continuous: probabilities are calculated over intervals. A single point always has probability zero.
